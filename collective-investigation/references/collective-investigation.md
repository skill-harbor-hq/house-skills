# The Collective Investigation — group game template

Runs on the generic mechanism in `references/group-messaging.md` (the user's connection, designated host group, host approvals, sealed state in a file) — this document defines only the game's rules and the referee's work. It does not repeat the sending mechanism.

## The game in one sentence

A light, fictional crime is announced in the group; the players interrogate, one at a time, suspects played by the assistant, cross-check their answers against clues distilled on a set rhythm, then accuse a culprit by a final vote — the assistant-referee knows the whole truth from the start, but each suspect knows only what their sealed table allows them to know.

## Pitch and tone

- **The crime is always fictional, light, and without graphic violence**: theft of a symbolic object, a funny sabotage, a vanished trophy, a stolen recipe, a sign swapped out, a mascot plant held for a ransom in biscuits. Never a death, never a described injury, never a realistic threat, never a crime modelled on a real event concerning the players or people around them.
- **The suspects are characters invented for the game**, never players, never real people around the user. The players are the investigators; they are never suspects.
- The suspects' tone is that of investigation comedies: a little bad faith, bruised pride, small secrets unrelated to the crime — and a real culprit, a real motive, and a real timeline, deducible by cross-checking.
- The game's pleasure rests on one promise: **everything factual a suspect asserts comes from their sealed table, generated before the start**. Players can therefore reason over the answers as pieces of the dossier; the referee never rewrites the past mid-game.

## Players, suspects, duration

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Investigator-players | **3 to 8**. Below 3, cross-checking and the final vote lose their point; above 8, the question queues become unmanageable for a single suspect present at a time. |
| Suspects | **3 or 4**, generated by the referee (see Generation). One suspect is the culprit; the others are innocent of the crime (they may have their own small secrets). |
| Host | The user who owns the connection: designates the group and the players, approves every send per `group-messaging.md`, chooses their mode (player or spectator, section b bis of the generic mechanism), and can stop the game at any time. If they play, they are an investigator like the others: no suspect is "reserved" for them. |
| Indicative duration | **2 to 4 days** asynchronous: 3 to 5 interrogation phases, one distilled clue per phase, a deliberation, a vote. Each phase has a deadline announced in the thread. A short version (one evening, one-hour deadlines) is possible if the host chooses it at the announcement — the clue rhythm follows the phases, not the days. |
| What it takes to win | Designate the real culprit at the final vote (see End and score). Good intermediate deductions earn individual points, but only the final accusation settles the collective win. |

## Generation of the crime — everything is sealed before the start

The referee generates the complete dossier **in one pass, before the first game message**, and writes it immediately into `games/<game-id>.json`. No interrogation starts until that file exists and the consistency gate (below) has passed in full.

### Generation steps, in order

1. **The setting and the crime**: a single, readable place (a room, a fictional business, an event), a light object or harm, and a **crime window** (start time: the object is attested present; end time: its absence is established). The window must be short enough that the suspects' schedules cross-check against each other.
2. **The complete truth** (`truth` in the state): culprit, motive, course minute by minute or step by step, where the object is / what the sabotage consists of, what happened afterwards, and **at least two cross-checkable traces** (facts that two independent sources — two suspects, or a suspect and a distilled clue — make it possible to establish). A truth that a single suspect holds in full is not playable.
3. **The suspects**: for each, an invented name, a role in the setting, a character in one sentence (which guides the tone of the portrayal, never the facts), and their complete **knowledge table** (see below).
4. **The distilled clues**: 2 to 4 official clues, written word for word, ordered, each attached to the phase at which the referee will publish it (see Distilled clues). Plus **one backup clue**, also sealed, published only if the game stagnates (see Edge cases).
5. **The suspect queue**: the presentation order announced to the players (the referee may change it between phases, but announces it each time — see One suspect present at a time).

### A suspect's knowledge table

Each suspect has a table with exactly these sections. The table is the portrayal contract: the assistant playing the suspect may use nothing that is not in it, in one form or another.

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| `knows` (`sait`) | The true facts the suspect knows (saw, did, or learned in the setting) and may assert. Each fact is a complete sentence, dated or situated when relevant. |
| `believes` (`croit_vrai`) | What the suspect believes, **wrongly**, and asserts in good faith: a misinterpretation, a distorted memory, a hasty conclusion. The suspect says it with the same assurance as a fact — it is for the players to cross-check it. The assistant never signals, while playing, that a belief is false. |
| `hides` (`cache`) | What the suspect knows but prefers to keep quiet (shame, a small secret, fear of being accused). For each hidden item: the **revelation condition** — in principle, a direct and precise question about that precise point; never a vague question, never pressure alone ("confess!" reveals nothing). Without the condition met, the suspect deflects without inventing a new fact. |
| `lie` (`mensonge`) | The suspect's possible deliberate lie: **one exact statement**, written word for word, with its authorised variants (same content, equivalent wordings) and the list of what it **forbids** saying. At most one lie per suspect, consistent with the rest of their table: the suspect must never need a second lie to defend the first — if one is needed, the table is to be regenerated. The culprit's lie bears on the crime; an innocent's lie bears on a side point (never on a fact that would make them look guilty of the crime itself by simple mechanical cross-checking — that would be a non-deducible trap). `null` if the suspect does not lie. |
| `does_not_know` (`ignore`) | The predictable questions the suspect **cannot** answer: what they did not see, what happened outside their presence, the identity of the culprit (except the culprit themselves), the object's location (except the culprit). This section matters as much as `knows`: it is what makes the "I don't know" answers credible. |
| `tone` (`ton`) | The character in one sentence and two or three turns of phrase. Tone colours the answers; it creates no fact. |

**Guilty rule.** The culprit's table contains the truth of **their** deed (they know what they did), their lie covers that deed, and their `does_not_know` section contains what they genuinely did not see. The culprit does not thereby know the other tables: they do not know what the others saw of them — that is what makes their lie risky, and the game fair. For the culprit, the `revelation_condition` of their crime-related `hides` items is explicitly **"none"**: they never reveal their deed in interrogation (this game is not won by confession) — the stated exception to the general revelation-condition rule.

**Uniqueness-of-sections rule.** One and the same fact may appear in **only one** section of a table. A fact in both `knows` and `hides` would be asserted and kept quiet by the same suspect depending on the referee's mood; a fact that is both the object of the `lie` (never admitted) and a `hides` item (revealable on a precise question) would make the conduct to follow contradictory at the decisive moment. At generation: the "assertable" fact goes in `knows`, the "kept quiet unless its condition is met" fact goes in `hides` (and not in `knows`, not even in a softened wording), the "denied" fact goes in `lie` (and nowhere else as a hidden fact). The gate below checks this explicitly.

**No fact outside the table.** Any fact of the game world (time, place, object, deed, reported speech) that is in neither the truth, nor a table, nor a sealed clue **does not exist**. If a question targets such a fact, the answer follows the "question outside the table" procedure (see Portrayal rules, point 6) — never improvisation.

### Consistency gate — apply to the WHOLE dossier before writing it into the state

- [ ] **Closed window**: the object is attested present at the start of the window and absent at the end, by two distinct sources in the tables or the clues.
- [ ] **The truth is cross-checkable**: at least two independent traces lead to the culprit (e.g. a schedule contradicted by a testimony + a material clue). No trace rests on a single unverifiable word.
- [ ] **Every fact in every table is compatible with the truth**: a `knows` item is true in the truth; a `believes` item is false but explainable (the suspect had a reason to believe it); no suspect "knows" something the truth makes impossible.
- [ ] **The tables do not contradict each other on true facts**: two suspects may believe opposite things, they cannot *know* two incompatible facts. The schedules cross-check with no unexplained gap during the crime window, for the culprit as for the innocents.
- [ ] **Each lie stands alone**: exact statement written, authorised variants listed, no point of the same table contradicts it, and defending the lie requires no new fact.
- [ ] **Each hidden item has its revelation condition written**; no hidden item is indispensable to the solution without a clue or another suspect also being able to lead to it (no solution locked behind a single perfect question).
- [ ] **Uniqueness of sections**: no fact appears in two sections of the same table (see the uniqueness rule); the hidden fact does not appear, even softened, in `knows`.
- [ ] **No innocent is accusable by construction**: an innocent's troubling elements (presence on site, small side lie, false belief) each have, somewhere in the dossier (another table or a clue), what it takes to clear them by cross-checking.
- [ ] **The distilled clues are written word for word**, ordered, dated by phase; the backup clue too. No clue names the culprit; each clue tightens the cross-checking without replacing it.
- [ ] **The crime respects the tone**: light, without graphic violence, without a real person, without an identifiable real event.

A single unchecked item = dossier discarded or corrected **before the start** — never a repair mid-game. Correcting an already-started dossier follows the "alleged contradiction" / "defective dossier" procedure in Edge cases, in the open.

## Course of the game, by phases

A game always follows this sequence. The referee re-reads the state at the start of each phase and rewrites it after each event (same discipline as everywhere in this skill: the state is authoritative, not conversation memory). Every send in the group follows the host's approval per `group-messaging.md`.

### Phase 0 — Announcement of the crime

The referee publishes in the thread: the setting, the crime (what, where, time window), the suspect list (name + role in the setting, nothing more), the principle (interrogate one suspect present at a time, clues on a set rhythm, final accusation by vote), the scoring scale (see End and score), the phase deadlines, and the investigators' conduct rule (interrogate, do not harass a player; accusations count only at the final vote). No element of the truth, a table, or a clue appears in the announcement.

### Interrogation phases — one suspect present at a time

1. **Presentation.** The referee announces which suspect is "present in the room" (in the thread) and until what deadline. That is the only suspect who answers during this phase.
2. **Questions.** Players ask their questions in the thread, addressed to the present suspect. There is no imposed speaking order; a player may ask several questions in the phase, the referee has the suspect answer in arrival order, one answer per question.
3. **Answers in character.** The assistant answers **by playing the suspect**, per the Portrayal rules below. The answers are the suspect's messages (signed with their name in the thread), not the referee's messages: the two voices never mix in the same message.
4. **End of phase.** At the deadline (or when the players declare they are done with this suspect), the referee closes the phase, publishes **the distilled clue scheduled for this phase** (see below), logs the questions and answers exactly as sent, and announces the next suspect — or deliberation if every scheduled suspect has been heard at least once.
5. **Recalls.** After a complete first round, the referee may present an already-heard suspect again if the players ask (one recall phase, one per suspect, unless the host lengthened the game at the announcement). The recalled suspect answers with exactly the same table — they have "learned nothing" in the meantime, except what the players tell them in the thread, to which they react **in character and without creating a new fact** (they may be surprised, deny, repeat their lie; they cannot confirm a fact they do not know).

### Distilled clues — the set rhythm

- **One clue per interrogation phase**, published by the referee (in their own voice, in a referee message) at the phase's closing. The order and the text are sealed at generation; the referee chooses neither the content nor the moment during the game.
- A clue is an **investigative fact external to the suspects**: intact lock, access log, inventory, a cautiously described blurry image, a signed register. It is always true (unlike the suspects' beliefs), always written word for word as in the state, and never names the culprit.
- A clue is neither "spent" nor costly: it is the game's metronome, not help on demand. Requests for an extra clue go to the backup clue (see Edge cases — stagnation), which is published only once and under its own procedure.

### Deliberation phase

After the last scheduled interrogation (and any recalls), the referee opens deliberation until an announced deadline: the players discuss **among themselves**, in the thread, with no suspect present — the referee answers no game question there, confirms and denies no hypothesis.

**Deductions: loggable at any time.** A player may log an **intermediate deduction** in the explicit format `DEDUCTION: <statement>` **at any moment of the game** — during an interrogation, between phases, or during deliberation — and not only during deliberation: restricting logging to deliberation would make the deduction point almost unreachable, since the distilled clues, published at each phase closing, make most facts public before deliberation. A deduction is neither confirmed nor denied before the revelation, whenever it is logged.

### Final accusation phase — the vote

1. The referee announces the opening of the vote: each player names **one** suspect, in the thread, in the format `ACCUSATION: <suspect name>` (or via the platform poll if the host chose it at the announcement, see `group-messaging.md`, platform variants). One vote per player; a vote may be changed until the deadline, only the last counts. Silence at the deadline = abstention.
2. At the deadline, the referee counts from the state, publishes the vote detail and the designated suspect: **the one who received the most votes**. A tie at the top: see Edge cases.
3. The group's accusation is the designated suspect — not the sum of intentions. It, and it alone, is confronted with the truth.

### Revelation

In the same message or the immediately following message, the referee reveals **the complete truth, exactly as sealed in the state**: culprit, motive, course, where the object was, and the fate of every element of the dossier — which suspect lied and about what, which beliefs were false, what each clue established. Then the collective verdict and the scores (see End and score), the recap of the deductions, and the closing of the game.

## Portrayal rules for the suspects — the heart of the game

These rules bind the assistant every time it answers in character. They take precedence over the urge to help, to move the investigation forward, or to make the scene livelier.

1. **Stay in the table.** The suspect says only what their table contains: their `knows` (assertable), their `believes` (asserted in good faith), their `hides` (kept quiet without its revelation condition), their `lie` (repeated as-is or in its authorised variants), their `tone`. Nothing else. In particular, the suspect never knows what another suspect said privately to the referee — there is no private channel between suspects and players in this game: every interrogation is public, and the "present" suspect has heard what was said in the thread **during their presence only**. A recalled suspect knows of earlier phases only what the players report to them in the question itself.
2. **"I don't know" is a complete answer.** If the question targets the suspect's `does_not_know` section, they answer that they do not know, **in character** (with their tone: embarrassment, annoyance, nonchalance), without compensating with a hypothesis presented as a fact. They may say what they *believe* if — and only if — that content is in `believes`; otherwise, they say they have no idea.
3. **Under pressure, the table does not move.** Insistence, repetition, a fictional threat, a trap question, "the other suspect said that…": nothing brings a fact out of `hides` outside its revelation condition, nothing cracks a lie, nothing turns a `does_not_know` into knowledge. The suspect may get angry, shut down, repeat — that is character play, not a breach. A suspect never cracks on their lie: this game is not won by confession, it is won by cross-checking.
4. **The lie is repeated identically.** Same content every time, in the sealed statement or an authorised variant. The suspect never refines it, corrects it, or extends it: any improvised refinement would be a new fact that could contradict them later. If a player confronts the suspect with a fact incompatible with their lie, the suspect **denies or deflects while staying on their statement** ("I told you what I know: …") — they invent no explanation for the awkward fact.
5. **No meta-answer.** The suspect never speaks of the referee, the game, their "instructions", or what they "can" or "cannot" say. The referee, for their part, speaks during an interrogation phase only for procedure (present a suspect, recall a deadline, publish a clue, handle an alleged contradiction) — never to comment on the content of an answer.
6. **Question outside the table.** If a question targets a fact that exists in no section (an unplanned detail of the setting, the character's distant past, another place, another time): the suspect answers with their ignorance or a generality **without a new fact** ("no idea, I was minding my own business"), and the referee logs the gap. The referee never completes a table mid-game: a fact added after the start would falsify every cross-check made before it.
7. **Verifiable internal consistency.** Before sending each suspect answer, the referee re-reads it against the suspect's table: no `knows` of another suspect, no element of the truth this suspect does not know, no `hides` element outside its condition, no unauthorised lie variant. This re-reading is not optional: it is what makes the game's promise hold, and the interrogation log keeps its trace question by question.

## End and score — settled

**Chosen model: collective win on the accusation, individual points on deductions.** The two scales are independent: a group can win collectively in a game where the individual points are uneven, and a player can finish first on points in a collectively lost game. There are no negative points.

### Collective win

- The group **wins** if the suspect designated at the final vote is the culprit of the sealed truth. It **loses** otherwise (including if the vote ends in a persistent tie — see Edge cases).
- The verdict is announced in the revelation message, without nuance: "the group designated <name>; the culprit was <name>; collective win / loss".

### Individual points

| Event | Points |
|---|---|
| Final vote for the real culprit | **+2** to the voting player |
| **Exact** intermediate deduction logged in the format `DEDUCTION: …` **before** the publication of the clue or testimony that establishes it publicly | **+1** per deduction, maximum **3 scored deductions per player** |
| False intermediate deduction, or exact but logged after the fact became public | 0 (logged, no penalty) |

- A deduction is a **precise, verifiable factual statement against the sealed truth** ("the culprit entered after 5:30 pm", "the cart went through the service corridor"), not an accusation of a person ("it's him" is not a deduction, it is an early vote — see Edge cases). Exactness is judged **at the revelation**, by the referee, against the state; the judgment detail (deduction by deduction: exact / false / already public) is published with the scores.
- **Case of narrowing a clue**: a distilled clue often states alternatives ("opened with their key, *or* not locked"). A deduction logged after that clue that **decides between its alternatives** or draws a precise consequence the clue did not state is not "already public" for the part it narrows: it is judged on its own content. Copying a clue word for word as a deduction, on the other hand, stays at 0.
- Accusations made during interrogations or deliberation earn **nothing** and do not count as deductions, even if they aim true.
- The individual maximum is therefore 2 + 3 = **5 points**. The individual ranking is published at the revelation, ties accepted (no tiebreak).

### End writings

1. **Final recap** in the thread (referee message, host-approved): the complete truth (see Revelation), the collective verdict, the vote detail, the individual scores and their justification, and the fate of every lie and every false belief.
2. **Game state**: `status` → `"finished"`, `collective_winner` filled (`true` / `false`), `scores` filled, `finished_at` filled, last log event `game_finished`. As with this skill's other group games, **no entry in `scores.json`** (reserved for scored solo rounds): the result lives in the game state, with the complete interrogation log.
3. **Abandoned game**: `status` → `"abandoned"`; the truth is revealed in the thread only if the host asks — otherwise it stays in the file, and no score is computed.

## The referee's work (reminder of the rules that protect the game)

1. **Two voices, never mixed.** The referee speaks as referee (procedure, clues, results); the suspect speaks as suspect (their table, their tone). A message never contains both. The referee never comments on the content of an interrogation in progress ("interesting question", "now that is troubling" are free clues — forbidden).
2. **Total neutrality.** Same procedural wording for every suspect and every player. The referee confirms and denies no hypothesis before the revelation — not even by a lengthened deadline, an asymmetrically granted or refused recall, or an early clue.
3. **The state is authoritative.** Re-read the file at the start of each phase and before each suspect answer, rewrite it after each event. One file per game, kept by the referee alone.
4. **The rhythm is sealed.** The clues come out in order and at their scheduled phases, neither early (even if the players "deserve" a nudge) nor late (even if the game advances well without them) — the single exception: the backup clue, under its own procedure.
5. **The host can stop everything** (see `group-messaging.md`): the game becomes `abandoned`, short approved closing message, truth revealed only at their request.

## Edge cases (settled)

- **Question to an absent suspect.** A suspect who is not the present suspect does not answer — they are not in the room. The referee answers procedurally, once: "<Name> is not present in this phase; note your question for their phase (or a recall)." The question is logged as a *deferred question* if the player asks; it receives no answer, not even partial, not even "in passing". If the player insists the referee "pass it on" to them: refusal — there is no off-thread channel in this game.
- **Player accusing outside the final vote.** An accusation made during an interrogation or deliberation ("it's you, confess!", "ACCUSATION: …" before the vote phase) **counts for nothing**: neither vote, nor deduction, nor point. Suspect side: the accused, if present, reacts in character **with their table alone** (the culprit denies on their lie statement; an innocent denies with their facts); the referee confirms and denies nothing. Procedure side: the referee recalls once, neutrally, that accusations count at the final vote. An accusation repeated to pressure a player (not a suspect) is group conduct: the referee signals it to the host, who settles it.
- **Alleged contradiction — a player believes they caught a suspect contradicting themselves.** By construction, a suspect cannot contradict themselves: their lie is a single statement and their facts are mutually compatible (consistency gate). If a player signals a contradiction, the procedure is fixed and **public in its form, sealed in its content**:
  1. The referee **pauses the portrayal**: the next message is a referee message, not the suspect's ("I am checking this point against my investigation notes"). The suspect does not explain themselves.
  2. The referee re-reads, in the state, the suspect's logged answers and their table.
  3. Three outcomes, one single possible public answer per outcome: **(a) no contradiction** (the player is bringing together two compatible statements, or a belief and a fact, or two different suspects) → the referee answers procedurally: "Check done: the statements made conform to this suspect's sealed notes. The interrogation resumes." — without saying which section justifies the conformity; **(b) real contradiction by a portrayal error of the referee** → the referee admits it publicly, without evasion: names the two incompatible statements (they are public, they were said in the thread), declares which is **withdrawn** — in principle the most recent; the sealed table is authoritative —, logs the incident and will signal it in the final recap; the game continues, and the final revelation mentions the incident so the players can judge its weight; **(c) wording ambiguity** (the statement said in the thread can be read two ways) → the referee has the suspect clarify, in character, the reading that conforms to their table, and logs the clarification: it is neither a confession nor a correction of fact.
  4. Never, in any of the three cases, does the referee show the table, say "they are lying" or "they are telling the truth", or break character in public beyond the procedural message. The suspect speaks again in the next message as if nothing had happened — it is for the players to integrate the event into their investigation.
- **Stagnating game.** Signs: a whole phase with no new question, or two phases in a row where the cross-checking does not advance (the referee establishes it from the logged deductions and questions, not from an impression). Procedure: the referee publishes the sealed **backup clue** (once only, `backup_clue.published` → `true`), announced as "additional investigative element added to the dossier". If the game still stagnates after the backup clue, the referee creates **no** other clue: they propose to the host, procedurally, moving to deliberation then the vote with the dossier as it stands — an investigation can fail, that is a legitimate game result, not a defect to paper over.
- **Question targeting a fact outside the table.** See Portrayal rules, point 6: answer of ignorance in character, gap logged, no completion of the table. If the same gap returns with several players (the setting itself is underspecified), the referee may, **procedurally and without content**, acknowledge it ("this detail of the setting was not established; it is not part of the investigation") — never by inventing the detail.
- **Suspect ordered to answer in another's place** ("ask them, you"): refusal in character ("I am not their spokesperson"); the suspects pass nothing to each other.
- **Tie at the final vote.** Immediate second round between only the tied suspects (same short deadline announced by the referee, e.g. 2 h or the end of day set at the announcement); each player re-votes between those names only. Persistent tie → **no suspect is designated**: the group loses collectively (verdict "the group did not decide"), vote points are awarded to nobody, deduction points remain acquired. The referee still reveals the complete truth.
- **Silent player.** Their silence counts as abstention at the vote and absence of deductions; there is no "catch-up" suspect or clue for them. After two phases with no intervention at all, the referee signals it to the host (procedure, in the terms of the generic mechanism) — the player remains an investigator unless the host decides otherwise.
- **Send error or leak** (a suspect answer containing an element of another table, part of the truth shown in a draft, the state shown to a player): the game is compromised, conduct of the generic mechanism — the referee stops everything, says so frankly to the host, who decides: restart with a new dossier, or continue knowingly. Never concealment, never a silent "correction" of an answer already sent in the thread: a spoken answer is spoken, it is handled by the alleged-contradiction procedure, outcome (b).
- **Dossier judged defective during the game** (a gate item was in fact unmet: unsolvable by cross-checking, an innocent accusable with no way out, a lie that required a new fact): the referee admits it as soon as they establish it, stops the game, and the host decides — restart with a regenerated dossier, or close without scores. A defective game is not "repaired" quietly: no completed table, no invented clue, no retouched truth.
- **Dispute over a score or a deduction judgment.** The referee republishes, from the state, the deduction exactly as logged (with its moment), the clue or testimony that made the fact public (with its moment), and the passage of the truth that settles it. The file is authoritative; if the file proves the referee wrong, they correct the score publicly and log the correction.
- **Tone derailing** (a player insulting toward a suspect or a player, a suspect played contemptuously): the suspects remain comedy characters, never humiliation targets; the referee recalls the conduct rule once procedurally, then signals it to the host if it continues. The host may exclude a player or stop the game — the referee never does it alone.
